Young adults who cut back on social media more likely to see symptoms ease

In a US study of 2,358 young adults with high symptoms and frequent social media use, those who cut back were more likely to report fewer symptoms later.

23 November 2026 · 3 min read

Much of the debate about social media and mental health asks whether heavy use is harmful. A new study asks a slightly different question: when young people who are already struggling cut back, do their symptoms tend to improve?

The research was published online in JAMA Pediatrics on 16 November 2026. It was led by Dae-Hee Han of Emory University in Atlanta, with Katherine Keyes of Columbia University and Megan Moreno of the University of Wisconsin-Madison.

What was studied

The team used data from the Population Assessment of Tobacco and Health (PATH) Study, a long-running US national survey. It was designed mainly to study tobacco use but also asks about social media and mental health. The researchers used survey waves from 2013 to 2021.

They focused on a high-risk group: young adults aged 18 to 24 who used social media several times a day and reported all four symptoms on a short mental health screener. The four symptoms covered depression, anxiety, psychological distress and sleep difficulties.

The researchers then followed these people across three survey waves at a time. They looked at whether someone had cut back to using social media once a day or less at the next wave, and whether their symptoms were still elevated at the wave after that. Symptoms counted as no longer elevated if a person reported three or fewer of the four.

The analysis included 2,358 young adults, giving 3,788 observations because some people appeared in more than one set of waves. Of these, 64.7% were female.

What it found

  • About 14% of observations (540) involved someone who moved to less frequent social media use.
  • Among those who cut back, 57% no longer had elevated symptoms at the later wave, compared with 49% of those who kept using social media several times a day.
  • The researchers estimated the difference at 7 percentage points. Put another way, those who cut back were about 15% more likely to no longer have elevated symptoms (prevalence ratio 1.15).
  • When the researchers counted symptoms rather than using a cut-off, cutting back was linked to fewer symptoms on average.

The results were broadly similar when the researchers tested their analysis in other ways, such as defining "cutting back" as using social media weekly or less, or handling missing data differently.

The authors concluded that their findings "identify reduced social media use as a potentially modifiable target for prevention and early intervention in this high-risk population."

What it can't tell us

This was an observational study. It shows that cutting back went with later improvement, but it can't show that cutting back caused it. Something else, such as a change in life circumstances, could have led to both less social media use and better mood.

Other limits:

  • Both social media use and symptoms were self-reported in a survey.
  • The study measured how often people used social media, not what they did on it or for how long.
  • It only included US young adults who already had high symptoms and used social media frequently, so the results may not apply to other groups.
  • The symptom screener is a brief questionnaire, not a clinical diagnosis.

The authors reported no conflicts of interest.

Practical context

The study didn't test any way of cutting back, so these are general points, not findings from the research:

  • If you notice that time on your phone or social media leaves you feeling worse, it may be worth paying attention to when and how you use it.
  • Low mood and worry can also change how people use their phones, so the link may run in both directions.
  • If low mood, anxiety or poor sleep has lasted a few weeks or is affecting daily life, talk to a doctor or qualified professional.
