ADHD medicines in pregnancy not linked to more neurodevelopmental conditions in children, large study finds

A study of 3.6 million children in Denmark, Norway and Sweden found no raised risk of neurodevelopmental conditions after ADHD medicine use in pregnancy.

28 November 2026 · 3 min read

People who take medicine for ADHD and become pregnant often face a difficult question: could the medicine affect how their child's brain develops?

A large study published in The BMJ on 25 November 2026 looked at this question using health records from three Nordic countries. The research team was based in Norway, Sweden, Denmark, Australia and Iceland, and the corresponding author is at the Norwegian Institute of Public Health.

What was studied

The researchers used nationwide registers in Denmark, Norway and Sweden covering births, medicines collected from pharmacies and diagnoses made in specialist healthcare.

They included 3,643,376 children born between 2000 and 2021. Children were followed for a median of 9 years in Sweden and 10 years in Norway and Denmark.

A child counted as "exposed" if their mother filled at least one prescription for an ADHD medicine during pregnancy. The team also looked separately at prescriptions filled in the second or third trimester, and at three individual medicines: methylphenidate, amphetamines and atomoxetine. Children whose mothers filled no ADHD prescriptions in pregnancy were the comparison group.

The outcome was any neurodevelopmental condition diagnosed in specialist healthcare, as well as groups of conditions and individual conditions. The researchers used statistical methods to account for differences between the two groups of mothers, known as confounders.

What it found

  • 7,595 children (0.2%) were exposed to an ADHD medicine before birth: 5,230 to methylphenidate, 1,510 to amphetamines and 553 to atomoxetine only.
  • About one third (2,389) were exposed in the second or third trimester.
  • After accounting for confounders, exposure was not linked to a higher risk of any neurodevelopmental condition (hazard ratio 1.05, 95% confidence interval 0.98 to 1.12).
  • There was also no raised risk of behavioural and emotional disorders (1.04, 0.97 to 1.12) or of disorders of psychological and intellectual development (1.06, 0.96 to 1.18).
  • This held for exposure at any time in pregnancy and in the second or third trimester, and for ADHD medicines overall and by type.

A hazard ratio of 1 would mean no difference in risk. In each case the confidence interval includes 1, so the study did not find a clear difference.

The authors concluded that the findings "strengthen existing evidence and can assist pregnant people and clinicians in weighing the benefits and risks of such treatment during pregnancy."

What it can't tell us

This was an observational study, so it shows links rather than proving cause and effect, and some confounding may remain.

The authors note that a few raised hazard ratios were seen for less common outcomes, but these were not found consistently across their analyses.

Exposure was based on prescriptions filled at a pharmacy, and diagnoses came from specialist healthcare records. The study looked at neurodevelopmental conditions in children; it did not set out to answer other questions about pregnancy and ADHD medicines.

The authors declared that some of them have taken part in research projects funded by pharmaceutical companies, with funds paid to their institutions, and said these were unrelated to this study.

Practical context

The following are general points, not findings of the study:

  • Decisions about any medicine during pregnancy, or when planning a pregnancy, are best made with a doctor, midwife or other qualified professional who knows your situation. Don't stop or change a prescribed medicine without talking to them first.
  • Pregnancy and the months after birth can be a demanding time for mental health, whether or not you have ADHD.
