Hair pulling, skin picking and nail biting common in teenagers, and distressing for some

In a survey of 5,437 students aged 11 to 18 in England, 58.5% reported hair pulling, skin picking or nail biting, and 12.7% reported moderate to high distress.

27 November 2026 · 3 min read

Picking at your skin, pulling your hair or biting your nails are known to researchers as "body-focused repetitive behaviours". A new University of Oxford study asked how common they are among teenagers, how much they bother them, and what tends to go along with them.

It was published in BMJ Mental Health on 19 November 2026, led by Clare Mackay of Oxford's Department of Psychiatry with Mina Fazel and the OxWell Study Team.

What was studied

The researchers used the 2025 OxWell Student Survey, which ran between February and May 2025 in 49 secondary schools and further education colleges in England, most of them in Oxfordshire and Merseyside.

Some schools offered an optional extra module that asked whether students "sometimes pick, pull or bite" at their hair, skin or nails "in a way that feels difficult to control", how much this upset them, and whether it got in the way of everyday life.

The analysis included 5,437 students aged 11 to 18. They also answered questions about anxiety and depression symptoms, bullying, and any autism or ADHD diagnosis.

What it found

  • 58.5% reported at least one of these behaviours. Nail biting was most common (43.6%), followed by skin picking (31.3%) and hair pulling (14.5%).
  • The behaviours often went together: 84% of those who pulled their hair and 76% of those who picked their skin reported at least one other behaviour.
  • Most were not very bothered. Of those reporting a behaviour, 35.2% said it caused no distress and 42.6% only a little. But 22.2% said it upset them quite a lot or a great deal.
  • Across the whole sample, 12.7% had a behaviour that caused moderate to high distress, and 3.3% reported the highest level of distress.
  • At least one behaviour was reported by 45.6% of boys, 64.6% of girls and 84.2% of trans and gender-diverse students.
  • Distressing behaviours were linked to anxiety and depression symptoms: 6.3% of students in the normal range had them, compared with 34.2% of those in the clinical range.
  • They were also linked to bullying: 9.6% of students who reported no bullying, compared with 27.1% of those bullied about once a week or more.
  • Distressing behaviours were more common in neurodivergent students (around 20%, compared with 10% of neurotypical students), but this link was no longer statistically significant once other factors were taken into account.

Hair pulling and skin picking were linked to more distress than nail biting. The authors suggest that asking about distress, rather than the behaviour alone, could help spot young people who need support. As an example, they propose the question: "Do you ever pick, pull or bite at your hair, skin or nails in a way that upsets you or gets in the way of things you want to do?"

What it can't tell us

This was an observational, cross-sectional study, so it shows links, not causes. It can't tell us whether anxiety, low mood or bullying lead to these behaviours, or the other way round.

Only students who chose the optional module were included. This group had more girls and trans and gender-diverse students, and more anxiety and depression symptoms, than the full survey, so the authors avoid calling their figures "prevalence". The questions had not been formally validated and do not show whether anyone would meet criteria for a diagnosis.

The survey was funded by the NIHR Applied Research Collaboration Oxford and Thames Valley, with support from Liverpool City Council and Oxfordshire City Council. The authors declared no competing interests.

Practical context

The following are general points, not findings of the study:

  • Picking, pulling or biting now and then is very common and not, on its own, a sign that something is wrong.
  • If it is causing a lot of upset, leaving marks, or getting in the way of school or friendships, talking to a parent, a school nurse, a GP or another qualified professional is a good step.
