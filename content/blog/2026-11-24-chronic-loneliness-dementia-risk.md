Most people feel lonely at some point. A new study from France suggests that what may matter for brain health in later life is not whether loneliness happens, but whether it lasts.

The research was published online in *Aging & Mental Health* on 17 November 2026. It was led by Yvanna Simon, with co-author Karine Pérès, a senior researcher, at the Bordeaux Population Health Research Center (Inserm and the University of Bordeaux).

## What was studied

The team used data from a French study that followed 3,777 older adults, aged 65 and over, from 1988 to 2018.

The analysis had two phases. First, loneliness was checked with a single question at repeated visits over eight years. Based on their answers, people were placed in one of three groups:

- **Never lonely:** no loneliness at any visit (48.3% of participants)
- **Occasionally lonely:** loneliness at one or two visits (34.4%)
- **Chronically lonely:** loneliness at three or four visits (17.3%)

Second, the researchers looked at who developed dementia over the following 20 years. Only people who were still free of dementia at the end of the first eight years were included. By 1995–96, when this second phase began, 1,540 participants were eligible.

The analysis took account of age, sex, education, living situation, health status, medications and depression. People with early cognitive decline were excluded, and deaths were recorded to account for competing health risks.

## What it found

Of the 1,540 participants, 560 (36.4%) developed dementia during follow-up. The share differed between the groups:

- **Never lonely:** 33.1% (246 of 743)
- **Occasionally lonely:** 34.5% (183 of 530)
- **Chronically lonely:** 49.1% (131 of 267)

After adjustment, people with chronic loneliness had a **38% higher risk** of dementia than those who were never lonely (adjusted hazard ratio 1.38; 95% confidence interval 1.03 to 1.86). Occasional loneliness was **not** linked to a higher risk.

Living alone did not tell the whole story: 29.6% of the chronically lonely group did not live alone.

"While most people experience loneliness at some point in their lives, it is when loneliness becomes chronic and persistent that it may increase the risk of developing dementia," said Karine Pérès.

Yvanna Simon said that "Healthcare professionals, social workers, and policymakers should not exclusively focus on living arrangements as a proxy for social risk, but should also incorporate subjective experiences of connection and loneliness."

## What it can't tell us

This was an **observational** study. It shows a link between long-lasting loneliness and later dementia, but it cannot prove that loneliness caused the dementia. Other factors that weren't measured could play a part.

Loneliness was measured with a single question, so the study could not capture details such as the quality of people's relationships or whether they had meaningful activities. The chronically lonely group was also fairly small (267 people), and the confidence interval is wide, so the true size of the link is uncertain.

The participants were older adults in France who joined the study from 1988 onwards, so the results may not apply in the same way to other countries or to people who are older today. The figures are group averages and do not predict what will happen to any one person.

## Practical context

The study did not test ways to reduce loneliness, so these are general ideas rather than findings of the research:

- Feeling lonely now and then is common. The study's results point to loneliness that persists over years.
- Being around people and feeling connected are not the same thing. Someone can live with others and still feel lonely.
- If loneliness has been with you for a long time, or is affecting your mood, sleep or memory, it is worth talking to a doctor or qualified professional.
