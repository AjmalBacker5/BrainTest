Some people can shrug off not knowing what will happen next. Others find it deeply uncomfortable. Psychologists call this second tendency "intolerance of uncertainty", and it is thought to be one of the building blocks of anxiety. A new review suggests it has become more common among young adults at college over the past three decades.

The study was published online in *JAMA Psychiatry* on 18 November 2026. It was led by Jeroen van Baar of Boston University School of Public Health, with senior author Katherine Keyes of Columbia University Mailman School of Public Health, alongside researchers at the University of Regina in Canada and the National University of Singapore.

## What was studied

Intolerance of uncertainty is described in the paper as difficulty enduring the perceived absence of important information. It is usually measured with a questionnaire called the Intolerance of Uncertainty Scale, which comes in a long 27-item version and a short 12-item version.

An earlier meta-analysis had found that scores on the long version rose among US and Canadian undergraduates between 1992 and 2014. The new study updated that work and added studies using the short version.

The researchers looked for peer-reviewed, English-language studies of non-clinical undergraduate samples in the US or Canada that reported an average score. After screening 799 abstracts and reviewing 294 full texts, they combined the new studies with the earlier meta-analysis. The final analysis included **137 studies** with **37,298 participants**. The typical sample had an average age of about 20, and on average 69.5% of participants were female. The researchers registered their hypothesis before collecting the data.

## What it found

Average intolerance of uncertainty went up over time:

- On the long version (scored from 27 to 135), average scores rose by **0.66 points a year** between 1992 and 2023.
- On the short version (scored from 12 to 60), average scores rose by **0.60 points a year** between 2005, the first sample, and 2023.

When both versions were combined on a common scale, the rise was **three times faster** from 2012 to 2023 than from 1992 to 2011.

"IU is not itself a health outcome or a root cause of disease, but an intermediary cognitive process that contributes to the development of anxiety," said Katherine Keyes in the university's news release.

## What it can't tell us

This study tracked average questionnaire scores across many separate groups of students over time. It did not measure anxiety itself, and it cannot show that rising intolerance of uncertainty caused rising anxiety.

It also cannot explain *why* scores rose. In the news release, the researchers list possible influences, such as smartphone and social media use, academic pressure, loneliness, economic instability and the COVID-19 pandemic, but these were not tested in the study. "Any explanation will be strongest if it accounts for both the long-term upward trend since the 1990s and the inflection point observed around 2012," said Jeroen van Baar.

The studies only covered undergraduates in the US and Canada, mostly women. The results may not apply to young people who are not at college or who live in other countries. They also describe group averages and say nothing about any one person.

The study was funded by the US National Institute of Mental Health. One author reported personal fees from opioid litigation outside the submitted work. No other conflicts of interest were reported.

## Practical context

The following are general points, not findings of the study:

- Feeling uneasy when plans are unclear is a normal human reaction. It becomes more of a concern when it regularly stops you from doing things or leaves you feeling on edge.
- If worry or uncertainty is affecting your sleep, studies, work or relationships, it is worth talking to a doctor or qualified professional.
