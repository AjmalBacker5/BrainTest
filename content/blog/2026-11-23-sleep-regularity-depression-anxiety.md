Advice about sleep usually focuses on how many hours we get. A new study suggests that *when* we sleep may matter too. People whose sleep and wake times varied more from day to day reported more symptoms of depression, anxiety and loneliness.

The research was published in *Scientific Reports* on 12 November 2026 by researchers at the University of California San Francisco, the University of California San Diego and Baruch College, City University of New York.

## What was studied

The team used data from 22,894 adults in the TemPredict Study. Participants wore a sleep-tracking ring and filled in monthly online surveys. The sleep data covered January to October 2020, and the surveys ran from April to November 2020. Shift workers were excluded.

From the ring data, the researchers worked out each person's **Sleep Regularity Index**. This compares whether someone is asleep or awake at the same minute on consecutive days. A score of 100 means a perfectly consistent pattern, and lower scores mean a less regular one. Participants averaged 83.1.

Symptoms were measured with standard questionnaires: PROMIS scales for depression and anxiety, and the 3-item UCLA Loneliness Scale.

## What it found

Lower sleep regularity went with worse symptoms, and the pattern was graded: the more severe the symptoms, the lower the average regularity score.

- **Depression:** people with severe symptoms averaged **74.93**, compared with **83.74** for those within normal limits.
- **Anxiety:** **78.94** for severe symptoms, compared with **83.67**.
- **Loneliness:** **81.79** for those with elevated loneliness, compared with **83.75**.

The biggest gap was for severe depression, a difference of about 8.8 points. Each 5-point drop in regularity was linked to higher odds of being in a worse symptom category. This held after the researchers accounted for age, sex and average sleep length. In those adjusted models, the odds ratios ranged from 1.091 to 1.457 when all available days of data were used.

The researchers also looked at a single "typical week" before COVID-related restrictions began. That showed a similar pattern, though the links were generally weaker.

## What it can't tell us

This was an **observational** study, and it looked at sleep and symptoms over the same period. It shows a link but can't show which comes first. The authors say their analysis "did not determine whether lower sleep regularity contributes to worse mental health, whether mental health symptoms disrupt sleep regularity, or whether both processes occur."

Other limits:

- Most participants were ring users who tended to be White, middle-aged adults from North America and Europe, so the results may not apply to everyone.
- The data come from 2020, a period of unusual disruption to daily routines.
- The ring is a consumer device, not a medical one, and naps were not counted.
- The study was partly funded by the ring's manufacturer, and two authors have received consulting fees from the company.

The authors suggest sleep regularity could be a risk factor that is worth testing in future intervention studies. That testing has not happened yet.

## Practical context

The study didn't test any changes to sleep habits, so these are general points, not findings from the research:

- Going to bed and getting up at roughly the same times, including at weekends, is common sleep advice.
- Low mood, worry and loneliness can disrupt sleep themselves, so an irregular pattern may be a sign of how you're feeling as much as a cause.
- If poor sleep or low mood has lasted a few weeks or is affecting daily life, talk to a doctor or qualified professional.
